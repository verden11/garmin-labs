# Evidence Behind Four Everyday Wellness Nudges

## Q1: What temperature/precipitation/UV/wind comfort ranges do weather or clothing-advice apps and services commonly use to recommend how to dress?

### Takeaway
No single universal standard; de facto consensus: apps bucket "feels-like" temperature (not raw air temp) into layering tiers ~every 10–15°F/8°C. UV advice follows WHO/EPA 5-band Global Solar UV Index (Low/Moderate/High/Very High/Extreme) almost verbatim across services. Wind/precipitation = modifiers to feels-like number and footwear/outerwear choice, not independent thresholds with universally-cited cutoffs.

### Cited Findings
- Weather-outfit apps commonly bucket by "feels-like" temperature: above 27°C/80°F → breathable fabric, no layers; 18–27°C/65–80°F → one light layer; 10–18°C/50–65°F → added, removable mid-layer — [Clothes Forecast / Weather Fit synthesis via search](https://weatherfit.com/)
- A "useful outfit recommendation needs three inputs: temperature range (low, high, and feels-like), precipitation and wind (affecting shoes/outerwear), and the day's context" — apps recommend dressing for feels-like, not raw number: wind and humidity can shift it by several degrees — [Wardrowbe](https://wardrowbe.com/blog/what-to-wear-based-on-weather/)
- WHO: sun protection recommended when UV Index forecast 3 or higher; below UVI 2, risk of short- and long-term UV damage for very sensitive, fair-skinned people is "limited", normally no protective measures needed — [WHO: Radiation — the ultraviolet (UV) index](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radiation-the-ultraviolet-(uv)-index)
- WHO notes its own UVI≥3 threshold "was chosen without evidence," several studies found DNA damage below UVI 3, prompting calls for WHO to revise guidance — [WHO: Radiation — the ultraviolet (UV) index](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radiation-the-ultraviolet-(uv)-index)
- EPA/NWS Global Solar UV Index 5-band scale, developed 1994, confirmed via EPA official PDF guide: Low = 1–2, Moderate = 3–5, High = 6–7, Very High = 8–10, Extreme = 11+ — [EPA UV Index guide PDF](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/documents/uviguide.pdf); [EPA UV Index Scale page](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/uv-index-scale-0)
- EPA exact per-band protective wording (paraphrase-fetched, high confidence, not verbatim quote check against source HTML): "No protection needed" at Low; "Protection needed. Seek shade during late morning through mid-afternoon... apply broad-spectrum SPF-15 or higher... wear protective clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, and sunglasses" at Moderate–High; "Extra protection needed... If your shadow is shorter than you, seek shade" at Very High–Extreme — [EPA UV Index Scale](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/uv-index-scale-0)
- NOAA/NWS cold-weather dressing guidance: "Wear layers of loose-fitting, lightweight, warm clothing... Outer garments should be tightly woven, water repellent, and hooded... wear a hat... Mittens, snug at the wrist, are better than gloves" — [NWS Wind Chill safety brochure](https://www.weather.gov/media/owlie/wind-chill-brochure.pdf); [weather.gov wind chill chart](https://www.weather.gov/safety/cold-wind-chill-chart)
- Wind chill explicitly defined by NOAA as "feels-like" derived metric based on rate of heat loss from exposed skin due to wind and cold — not raw-temperature reading — [NOAA JetStream: Wind Chill](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/synoptic/wind-chill)
- AccuWeather RealFeel Temperature Guide publishes 18 distinct RealFeel bands (from "extraordinarily dangerous heat," 133–140°F, down to "extraordinarily dangerous cold," -120 to -91°F), each paired with clothing/activity guidance and stated health/safety impact — [AccuWeather RealFeel Temperature Guide press release](https://www.accuweather.com/en/press/85129613)

### Inferences
- **Descriptive-safe wording**: state feels-like number, named UV/wind-chill band ("Moderate," "High"), neutral layering language ("you may want a light jacket") = mirrors how EPA/WHO/NOAA/AccuWeather already word this; safe, established, non-medical framing.
- **Risk of crossing into a health claim**: EPA/NOAA language sometimes states explicit protective action ("protection needed," "seek shade," "cover your mouth to protect your lungs"); copy mimicking this imperative-safety phrasing (rather than "UV is high today") edges toward safety/health directive language. Purely descriptive copy should report band/number, let user infer action, not issue protective instruction verbatim.
- WHO's own caveat that UVI≥3 cutoff "was chosen without evidence" useful for hedging: product copy citing UV threshold should treat number as convention, not validated safety line.

- Could not fetch full NWS wind chill and heat index numeric tables directly (PDF/table content); only qualitative dressing guidance retrieved.
- Found no single named "industry standard" precipitation-probability threshold (e.g., "70%+ chance of rain → recommend an umbrella") from primary weather-service source; appears app-specific/proprietary, not standardized.

## Q2: What's the evidence behind "optimal time for a break or walk"?

### Takeaway
Two independent, well-evidenced mechanisms support break/walk nudge: (1) circadian post-lunch dip in alertness, robustly documented in chronobiology research to fall roughly 13:00–16:00 (peaking ~14:00–15:00), independent of whether lunch was eaten; (2) vigilance/attention decrement over sustained task time, underlying occupational-health "micro-break" guidance (OSHA: 5 min/hour) and popular but evidentially looser interval schemes (Pomodoro's 25 min; 90-minute "ultradian" break claim). 25-minute and 90-minute numbers widely repeated but not rigorously validated as universal biological cycles — matters for how confidently a product can cite them.

### Cited Findings
- Post-lunch dip is a "real phenomenon that can occur even when the individual has had no lunch and is unaware of the time of day," rooted in circadian system's 12-hour harmonic — i.e., not purely digestive — [Frontiers in Physiology / search synthesis of postlunch dip literature](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.3109/07420529609037076); [PubMed: Circadian determinants of the postlunch dip in performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8877121/)
- Dip typically described as occurring in "early afternoon," commonly cited 14:00–16:00, broader window of reduced alertness ~13:00–15:00 reported elsewhere — [PubMed: The post-lunch dip in performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15892914/); [ResearchGate: The Post-Lunch Dip in Performance](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7848298_The_Post-Lunch_Dip_in_Performance)
- Dip attributed in part to "a slight reduction in core body temperature, which promotes a tendency to sleep" — [PMC: Modeling Napping, Post-Lunch Dip, and Other Variations in Human Sleep Propensity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2647793/)
- OSHA's own eTool states: "Every hour, take a five-minute break from computer tasks. Look away, stretch, get up, or walk," framed as "micro break or rest pause" to prevent localized fatigue from sustained/repetitive computer tasks — [OSHA eTools: Computer Workstations, Work Process](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/work-process)
- OSHA explicit: ergonomic best-practice guidance, not legal requirement; OSHA regulations do not mandate rest or meal breaks for any number of hours worked — [OSHA Standard Interpretation, 1986-01-24](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1986-01-24); [Wikipedia: Break (work)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Break_(work))
- Pomodoro 25-minutes-on/5-minutes-off: inventor claims "20- to 45-minute time intervals can maximize our attention and mental activity, if followed by a short break," but independent commentary notes specific 25-minute figure "fixed... [as] a rule-of-thumb... rather than being based on specific research," and randomized-trial literature on work/break splits mostly used 24/6 and 12/3 ratios, not validating 25/5 specifically — [Todoist: The Pomodoro Technique](https://www.todoist.com/productivity-methods/pomodoro-technique); [Brown Daily Herald fact-check](https://www.browndailyherald.com/article/2026/03/fact-check-is-the-pomodoro-technique-actually-effective-for-studying)
- Vigilance decrement = well-established lab finding: "when people monitor a repetitive display or perform the same attention-heavy task for a long time, detections can fall, reactions can slow and errors can increase" — [ScienceBlog.com summary of ultradian/vigilance research](https://scienceblog.com/t-90-minutes-work-20-minutes-recovery-not-universal-brain-cycle/)
- "90 minutes work / 20 minutes recovery" ultradian-rhythm break routine explicitly described by same source as "a useful personal routine—but... not a universal brain cycle or biologically prescribed maintenance schedule" — [ScienceBlog.com](https://scienceblog.com/t-90-minutes-work-20-minutes-recovery-not-universal-brain-cycle/)

### Inferences
- **Descriptive-safe wording**: nudge like "It's early afternoon — a common dip in alertness" or "You've been active/still for a while — a short walk is often suggested around now" stays within well-documented circadian/occupational-health territory, does not diagnose individual user.
- **Health-claim risk**: specific numeric claims like "your attention drops every 90 minutes" or "take exactly a 5-minute break every hour or you risk injury" overstate what evidence supports as universal, individually-applicable rule — 90-minute ultradian claim and Pomodoro 25-minute figure both flagged by own proponents/critics as heuristics, not validated biological constants. Safer: cite post-lunch dip's approximate window (13:00–16:00) as population-level pattern, OSHA's "5 min/hour" only as descriptive of existing occupational guidance ("common ergonomic guidance suggests...") rather than prescription tailored to user's own physiology.

### Gaps
- Found no peer-reviewed primary source (vs. secondary blog/aggregator commentary) directly for "90-minute ultradian rhythm" claim; most detailed source (ScienceBlog.com) itself science-communication summary; weight its debunking framing over popular-productivity-blog claims of strict 90-minute cycle.
- Did not locate specific occupational-health body (beyond OSHA) with formal break-interval numbers for general "take a walk" nudge (e.g., WHO or NIOSH); OSHA ergonomics eTool strongest primary source found.

## Q3: What physiological signals do Garmin, Fitbit, Whoop, and Apple use to suggest a stress-reset moment, and how do they word such prompts?

### Takeaway
Garmin, Fitbit, Whoop all derive stress/readiness signal primarily from heart-rate variability (HRV) plus resting/elevated heart rate, sleep, recent exertion; each surfaces it as bounded score or qualitative band (not diagnosis) with companion breathing/relaxation prompt gated on detected rise or sustained-high reading. Apple notably does **not** publish comparable numeric stress score on Apple Watch — offers scheduled/user-configured Mindfulness reminders (Breathe/Reflect) decoupled from any physiological stress trigger; notable contrast in how conservatively a platform can word this without any diagnostic framing at all.

### Cited Findings
- Garmin's Stress Level score runs 1–100, derived from HRV data through day; "sustained high stress — from workouts, difficult conversations, or hours of focused work — drains Body Battery faster," explicitly generalizing across physical and cognitive stressors — [the5krunner: Garmin Body Battery](https://the5krunner.com/garmin-features/sleep/body-battery/); [Garmin support: What Is the Stress Level Feature on My Garmin Device?](https://support.garmin.com/en-US/?faq=WT9BmhjacO4ZpxbCc0EKn9) (support.garmin.com blocked automated fetch this session — see Gaps)
- Garmin's "Relax Reminder" = device feature that "alert[s] you when your stress level is unusually high," alert "encourag[es] you to take a moment to relax," prompting guided breathing exercise — [Garmin support: Relax Reminder Notification FAQ](https://support.garmin.com/en-US/?faq=iLx2XZQdfn9fNNu2AXq6a5) (page content summarized via search snippet; primary fetch blocked — see Gaps)
- Garmin vívoactive 5 owner's manual (primary source, successfully fetched): Body Battery calculated from "heart rate variability, stress level, sleep quality, and activity data," described with explicit metaphor "Like a gas gauge on a car, it indicates your amount of available reserve energy" — deliberately non-diagnostic framing — [Garmin vívoactive 5 Owner's Manual, Body Battery section](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-5D183A14-BB43-4A9B-B441-5F824214CE40/EN-US/GUID-87E1392B-2C55-40B7-A1FF-3AB9252DA0A0.html)
- Fitbit's Stress Management Score (legacy branding) ranges 1–100, built from three sub-scores: Responsiveness (HRV, elevated resting heart rate, sleeping heart rate above RHR, EDA), Exertion Balance (recent activity vs. over/under-exertion), Sleep Patterns (deep sleep, fragmentation, rolling weekly "sleep reservoir") — [Wareable: Fitbit stress score explained](https://www.wareable.com/fitbit/fitbit-brings-stress-score-to-all-devices-8394)
- Fitbit's official framing strictly descriptive/comparative, not diagnostic: "A higher score indicates that your body is better prepared to handle the physical and mental strain and stress of the day" — [Fitbit Help Center, redirected to Google Health support](https://support.google.com/googlehealth/answer/14237928)
- Under Google Health rebrand, same metric now called **"Resilience score"**, presented with **qualitative bands only — "Optimal," "Balanced," or "Low"** — not exposing raw 1–100 number to user; improvement guidance generic ("Get regular exercise"), not prescriptive — [Google Health support: Resilience score](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14237928)
- WHOOP's Stress Monitor gives "a real-time stress score between 0 and 3... comparing that to your baseline," labeled 0 = low stress up to 3 = "your peak stress level" — [Wareable/Kygo summary of WHOOP Stress Monitor](https://www.kygo.app/post/whoop-stress-score-recovery-explained); [WHOOP support: Get to Know the Stress Monitor](https://support.whoop.com/APP_FEATURES__COACHING/Understanding_Your_WHOOP_Features/Get_to_Know_the_Stress_Monitor) (primary fetch blocked — see Gaps)
- "When the Stress Monitor detects a prolonged period of high stress levels, it can send you a notification to help make you aware," and as scores climb, members "notified so that they can manage it in real-time via the app's breathwork protocols" (cyclic hyperventilation to increase alertness, or physiological sigh to increase relaxation) — [WHOOP press center: Introducing Stress Monitor](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/press-center/whoop-launches-new-stress-monitor-feature-first-wearable-to-measure-daily-stress-levels-and-implement-stress-reduction-interventions-in-real-time/)
- WHOOP's Recovery Score (0–100%) uses three color bands with explicit, non-alarmist descriptive text: Green (67–100%) "You are well recovered and primed to perform"; Yellow (34–66%) "Your body is maintaining and ready to take on moderate amounts of strain"; Red (0–33%) "Rest is likely what your body needs... Some reasons could include overtraining, sickness, stress, lack of sleep, or other lifestyle factors" — note Red explicitly lists multiple possible causes, not asserting one, avoiding diagnostic claim — [search synthesis, WHOOP Recovery Score](https://www.whoop.in/pages/how-it-works)
- Apple Watch has no published numeric "stress score." Closest analog = Mindfulness app (Breathe/Reflect), scheduled by user, not triggered by detected physiological stress signal: "The Mindfulness app on your Apple Watch encourages you to set aside a few minutes a day to focus, center, and connect as you breathe," with optional "Start of Day and End of Day" reminders user toggles on, plus ability to "Mute mindfulness reminders" — [Apple Support: Start a Reflect or Breathe session on Apple Watch](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/start-a-reflect-or-breathe-session-apd371dfe3d7/watchos)

### Inferences
- **Descriptive-safe wording**: all four platforms' safest language pattern = bounded score/band plus neutral behavioral option ("a moment to relax," "breathwork," a Breathe session), not assertion about cause. WHOOP's Red-band copy good model: names several possible contributing factors, not asserting cause is "stress" specifically. Garmin's "gas gauge" metaphor and Fitbit/Google's move to qualitative-only bands (hiding raw number) both suggest industry drift toward less precise, less clinical-sounding presentation over time.
- **Health-claim risk**: phrasing asserting *why* a score dropped ("you're stressed because...") or recommending action framed as medical necessity ("you need to rest") crosses from descriptive into diagnostic/prescriptive. Evidence shows established platforms avoid this — Garmin frames Body Battery purely as "reserve energy," not stress diagnosis; Fitbit/Google explicitly rebranded toward vaguer bands; WHOOP hedges Red/low band with multi-cause list.
- Apple's approach (no physiological trigger, no diagnostic score at all — just schedulable mindfulness reminder) = most conservative model available if product wants to entirely sidestep any claim about what sensor reading means.

- support.garmin.com and support.whoop.com both returned HTTP 403/401 to automated fetch this session, so *exact verbatim* text of Garmin's official Stress Level FAQ, Garmin's Relax Reminder FAQ, WHOOP's Stress Monitor support page could not be directly quoted — findings above rely on search-engine-synthesized snippets of those pages (attributed to correct URLs), not confirmed direct quote. Follow-up manual visit to these support URLs recommended before treating any of their wording as verbatim.
- Could not confirm precise on-device notification text (e.g., literal string shown on Garmin watch face or WHOOP app push notification) for either platform's stress alert — only paraphrased descriptions of behavior found.

## Q4: How do these wearable platforms present an evening energy/Body-Battery-style summary — a number/trend, or an imposed mood/verdict?

### Takeaway
Where primary source could be checked, all platforms lean toward presenting **number, band, or trend (charged vs. drained, percentage, qualitative tier) rather than mood or verdict statement about user's day**. Garmin's own manual language explicitly metaphorical/non-verdict ("reserve energy," "gas gauge"); Fitbit/Google's newer Resilience framing deliberately drops raw number for three neutral tiers; WHOOP's Recovery bands pair percentage with short capacity statement, not mood label.

### Cited Findings
- Garmin's Connect app shows Body Battery as level (5–100) plus separate "CHARGED" and "DRAINED" figures representing day's gains and losses (deltas), not single interpretive verdict — [Garmin Forums: Body Battery Charge discussion](https://forums.garmin.com/apps-software/mobile-apps-web/f/garmin-connect-mobile-andriod/353075/sleep-timeline-metrics-missing---body-battery-charge); [GitHub dbhq-uk/garmin-skill Issue #12, on charged/drained as deltas vs. levels](https://github.com/dbhq-uk/garmin-skill/issues/12)
- Garmin's owner's manual defines four Body Battery bands purely by number range and reserve-energy label, no evaluative language attached ("5 to 25 is very low reserve energy... 76 to 100 is high reserve energy") — [Garmin vívoactive 5 Owner's Manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-5D183A14-BB43-4A9B-B441-5F824214CE40/EN-US/GUID-87E1392B-2C55-40B7-A1FF-3AB9252DA0A0.html)
- Fitbit/Google Health's Resilience score presented to users as one of three words only — "Optimal," "Balanced," or "Low" — deliberately abstracting away underlying 1–100 number; framed around "capacity for the day ahead," forward-looking, non-evaluative statement, not verdict on day just finished — [Google Health support: Resilience score](https://support.google.com/fitbit/answer/14237928)
- WHOOP's Recovery Score pairs percentage with short, capability-framed sentence per color band (e.g., "primed to perform," "ready to take on moderate amounts of strain," "Rest is likely what your body needs"), not mood word ("good day," "bad day") — [search synthesis, WHOOP Recovery Score bands](https://www.whoop.in/pages/how-it-works)
- Apple does not publish end-of-day energy/Body-Battery-style summary at all on Apple Watch; closest equivalent, Mindfulness "Weekly Summary," = opt-in aggregate of mindful-minutes usage, not daily energy verdict — [Apple Support: Start a Reflect or Breathe session on Apple Watch](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/start-a-reflect-or-breathe-session-apd371dfe3d7/watchos)

### Inferences
- **Descriptive-safe wording**: showing number/percentage plus neutral trend indicator (up/down arrow, "charged +X / drained −Y," capacity-framed sentence like "ready for moderate activity") matches how all three number-publishing platforms (Garmin, Fitbit/Google, WHOOP) already word this. None label a day "good" or "bad" outright — describe *capacity*, not *verdict*.
- **Health-claim/editorializing risk**: any copy reducing summary to mood/verdict word ("Great day!" / "Rough day") = step beyond what any of four platforms' own documentation does. Safest anchor pattern across sources: number or band + capacity/readiness statement, optionally with multiple possible contributing factors (as WHOOP's Red band does), not single causal or evaluative claim.
- Industry trend (Fitbit's shift from bare number to three qualitative words) suggests *reducing precision* = more conservative, more common direction platforms move over time, not adding more diagnostic detail.
